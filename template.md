# GenAI Policy

_Note: Throughout this document, "you" refers to students enrolled in the course, "we" refers to examiners/supervisors/teaching assistants._

The goal of your {{PROJECT_TYPE}} is to demonstrate your ability to {{LEARNING_GOAL_DESCRIPTION}}.  
All GenAI use must align with the objectives and intended learning outcomes defined in the [course syllabus]({{SYLLABUS_URL}}).
GenAI should support, rather than replace, your demonstration of competencies such as critical thinking and scientific writing.

The guidelines below are written in a **"Do"/"Don't" format** to explain what you are allowed or encouraged to do and what you must not do.  
Each guideline includes a **Motivation** and an **Example** illustrating acceptable behavior.

---

# 1. General Principles

## 1.1 Full Responsibility and Accountability

- **Do**: Take total ownership of every word, figure, and line of code; verify all AI-assisted output against primary sources.
- **Don't**: Assume that the AI knows best (e.g., using “The AI said so” as a justification), that details do not matter, or that examiners will not notice.
- **Motivation**: You are effectively "signing" the report as your intellectual output.
  You must be able to explain, justify, and trace the origin of any part of it.
  In group work, accountability is collective; all authors are responsible for the entire content.
- **Example**: A student experimented with ChatGPT for structuring a report, found the output superficial, and started from scatch on his own.

## 1.2 Transparency

- **Do**: Openly discuss your GenAI workflows with your supervisor; discuss opportunities, limitations, and risks of specific use cases.
- **Don't**: Hide your use of tools or wait until the final submission to disclose them.
- **Motivation**: Pragmatically, this policy document cannot cover every use case.
  More importantly, transparency is a sign of academic maturity and professional integrity.
  We highly value and appreciate being careful and reflective about GenAI use. 
- **Example**: Show your supervisor the prompts you used to brainstorm a research methodology and explain how you worked with the ideas.

---

# 2. Guidelines Throughout the Process

## 2.1 Related Work & Conceptual Understanding

- **Do**: Use AI-supported search engines (e.g., Scopus AI) for initial literature discovery and chat-based systems to discuss and explore well-established concepts (e.g., "Explain {{COURSE_CONCEPT}} to me like I am 10 years old.").
- **Don't**: Delegate the judgment of paper credibility and relevance to AI, rely on AI summaries of papers, or cite papers you haven't read yourself; cite AI as a source, or even use generated explanations in your writing (see also Guideline 2.2 on Writing & Communication below).
- **Motivation**: Scientific integrity requires citing identifiable sources. AI systems may hallucinate references or misrepresent papers.
- **Example**: Use Scopus AI to find "papers about {{COURSE_CONCEPT}}", but read the full papers yourself to decide if they fit your research gap.

## 2.2 Writing & Communication

- **Do**: Use language tools (e.g., grammar or translation support) to refine text you have already written. (Attention: Chat-based systems like ChatGPT on the other hand require you to be more careful because they are trained to please their users and willingly offer to do your work for you!)
- **Don't**: Provide notes or bullet points to an AI and ask it to generate paragraphs or entire sections; use terminology that sounds professional or scientific but you don't understand.
- **Motivation**: Not only are AI-generated texts often lifeless and plain boring to read, but converting ideas into academic prose is a core skill that you need to demonstrate in this course.
- **Example**: Write a draft of your "Related Work" section yourself, then use an LLM-based system to check for subject-verb agreement, consistent use of the tenses, or more natural sentence transitions.

## 2.3 Technical Work & Data Handling

- **Do**: Use AI to help debug code snippets or understand software libraries.
- **Don't**: Upload confidential, proprietary, or personal data to public AI systems.
- **Motivation**: You must comply with GDPR and intellectual-property agreements.
- **Example**: Ask an AI why a SQL query is slow, but don't include the actual database schema or sensitive user records.

---

# 3. Documentation and Consequences

## 3.1 Mandatory Disclosure

- **Do**: Clearly report all AI tool usage in all submissions; include prompts and outputs in an appendix.
- **Don't**: Omit reporting tool usage for "minor" tasks such as language polishing.
- **Motivation**: Academic integrity requires a clear trail of how the work was produced.
- **Example**: Include a statement such as:  
  _“Grammarly was used for language refinement; Scopus AI was used for initial literature discovery, prompts and outputs are documented in Appendix B; ...”_

## 3.2 Verification and Disciplinary Actions

- **Oral Follow-Up**: The examiner may conduct an oral follow-up with in-depth questions for any written part. Failure to demonstrate understanding results in a Fail grade.
- **Disciplinary Board**: In addition to an oral follow-up, we will report suspected misuse of GenAI, i.e., beyond the guidelines defined above, to BTH's Disciplinary Board. They will then conduct interviews with you and may sanction a violation of the rules with a suspension, meaning no campus and library access, blocked university account, no student support, not allowed to attend lectures or to be assessed and gain credits (in any course).
- **Example**: A student who included an AI-generated table in a report for a written assignment (different course) was suspended for 6 weeks at the end of 2025.
